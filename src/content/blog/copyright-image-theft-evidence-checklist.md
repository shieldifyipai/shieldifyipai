When a photograph, illustration, product image, or graphic appears on an unauthorized page, the first task is not drafting a notice. It is building a record that connects the protected work, the rights owner, the copied use, and the relevant context.

## Identify the original work precisely

Create a controlled asset record containing the original file, title or internal identifier, creator, creation date, publication history, ownership information, licenses, and available registration details. Preserve the highest-quality source and its file metadata.

Avoid relying only on a public webpage as proof of the original. Websites change, and the uploader may not be the legal owner.

## Document the ownership chain

Copyright may initially belong to an individual creator and later be assigned, licensed, or created under an employment or commissioned-work arrangement. Record contracts, assignments, employment context, agency relationships, and the exclusive rights relied upon.

The [U.S. Copyright Office overview](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/) provides general information about copyright authorship, ownership, and protected works. Requirements vary by jurisdiction and facts, so uncertain chains should be reviewed by qualified counsel.

## Capture the copied use in context

Record the complete URL, page title, site or account owner, seller identity where applicable, date, time, and how the image is used. Preserve full-page screenshots and focused captures showing the copied material.

Also record:

- Product, article, advertisement, or profile context.
- Captions, credits, watermarks, and copyright notices.
- Image URL and displayed dimensions.
- Links, downloads, or commercial offers associated with the image.
- Related pages using the same asset.

Do not crop the evidence so tightly that a reviewer cannot understand where the image appeared.

## Compare versions carefully

Keep the original and observed copy side by side. Note cropping, rotation, overlays, color changes, background removal, text additions, or partial use. Similarity tools can assist discovery, but their scores should remain review signals.

If the work contains third-party elements, identify which portion the organization owns or controls.

## Check authorization and exceptions

Search internal license records, agencies, syndication partners, affiliates, stock libraries, and customer permissions. Consider whether the use may involve an applicable exception, limitation, public-domain material, or another legitimate basis.

Do not assume lack of a visible credit means lack of permission. Conversely, giving credit does not itself establish authorization.

## Prepare the response record

Before submitting a platform or service-provider request, confirm the claimant, authority to act, original work, specific copied URLs, good-faith basis, required statements, and signature requirements. The U.S. Copyright Office describes the DMCA notice-and-takedown framework as one route for qualifying online contexts, but different services and jurisdictions may require different processes.

Record the exact notice, submission date, reference number, response, counter-notice, retraction, and final status.
