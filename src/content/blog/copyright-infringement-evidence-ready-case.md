When creative content is copied online, the strongest first response is usually not the fastest complaint. It is a clear record connecting the original work, the rights holder, the suspected copy, and the requested action.

Copyright rules, exceptions, registration requirements, and platform procedures vary. This workflow is designed to improve operational readiness, not to determine whether a particular use is legally infringing.

## Understand what the case is about

Copyright protects original expression, not every idea, fact, title, method, or system. The [U.S. Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/) describes copyright as protection for original works of authorship fixed in a tangible form and lists examples including photographs, illustrations, music, software, books, blog posts, and films.

Before opening a case, identify the specific material at issue:

- A product photograph or image set.
- Written product descriptions, articles, or training materials.
- Video, audio, music, animation, or advertising creative.
- Software code, interface artwork, or downloadable files.
- Illustrations, designs, catalogs, or presentation materials.

Avoid describing the case only as “they copied our website.” Isolate the individual works and the particular uses that require review.

## Build the ownership package

An enforcement reviewer may need to understand who created the work, who owns it now, and who is authorized to act. Keep a structured ownership package containing the records available for the relevant work.

Depending on the situation, that package may include:

- Original project files and creation dates.
- Publication records and first-known public URL.
- Copyright registration information where applicable.
- Employment, assignment, licensing, or commissioned-work records.
- Creator and rights-holder identity.
- Authorization for an agency, employee, or representative to submit a report.

Do not send unnecessary confidential material to a platform. Select the records required by the relevant process and preserve the fuller ownership file internally.

## Capture the original work in context

Save more than a cropped image of your own content. Record where the work appeared, when it was published, and how it connects to the rights holder.

Useful original-work evidence may include:

1. The live source URL and a full-page capture.
2. Original files with available metadata.
3. Publication or upload dates.
4. Product identifiers, catalog references, or campaign records.
5. Archived or internal records showing earlier creation or publication.

Keep unmodified originals. If you create a comparison image or add annotations, store it separately from the source evidence.

## Document the suspected copy

For the external use, capture enough information for a reviewer to locate and understand the content even if the page later changes.

Record:

- Complete URL, account name, seller name, or app identifier.
- Full-page screenshots plus detailed captures of the copied material.
- Date, time, time zone, and capture method.
- Page title, product identifier, description, price, and availability where relevant.
- Links to downloads, mirrors, embedded media, or connected profiles.
- Any statements suggesting ownership, authorization, or affiliation.

If there are multiple URLs, use one row per URL in the case record. Large unstructured lists make it harder to track platform responses and may hide duplicate or inactive locations.

## Prepare a clear comparison

A reviewer should not have to guess which parts correspond. Create a comparison that identifies:

- The original work.
- The suspected copy.
- The matching elements.
- Material differences.
- The source URL for each item.

Avoid overstating the comparison. Similar subject matter does not automatically mean protected expression was copied. Context, licensing, exceptions, and jurisdiction-specific rules may affect the analysis.

## Review authorization and possible exceptions

Before escalation, check internal licensing and distribution records. The use may come from an authorized distributor, former campaign, syndication agreement, affiliate, stock-content license, or customer permission that is not visible to the monitoring team.

Also flag facts that may require legal review, such as criticism, commentary, education, news reporting, parody, quotation, or other potential limitations and exceptions. Do not treat an automated similarity score as a legal conclusion.

## Match the evidence to the reporting channel

Platforms, hosts, search services, and marketplaces use different forms and require different information. A submission may ask for:

- Rights-holder and representative details.
- Identification of the copyrighted work.
- Exact location of the reported material.
- Statements of good-faith belief and accuracy.
- Electronic signature and contact information.

Read the current form carefully and submit only after the evidence and authority are confirmed. Track the submission reference, date, status, response, requested follow-up, and final outcome.

## Preserve a defensible case timeline

A useful timeline begins before the report and continues after it:

1. Original creation or publication.
2. First detection of the external use.
3. Evidence capture.
4. Ownership and authorization review.
5. Submission or outreach.
6. Platform response and any counter-notice or appeal.
7. Resolution, closure, or escalation.

This history supports internal reporting and helps teams respond consistently if the same content reappears on another account or channel.

## Evidence-ready case checklist

- The exact original work is identified.
- Ownership and authority records are available.
- Original publication context is preserved.
- Every reported URL has its own evidence.
- Comparisons are factual and clearly labeled.
- Licenses and possible exceptions have been reviewed.
- Submission references and outcomes are tracked.
