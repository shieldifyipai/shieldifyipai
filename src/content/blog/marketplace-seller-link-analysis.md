Marketplace reviews often reveal several storefronts with similar products, images, contact details, or naming patterns. Link analysis helps investigators organize those overlaps. It should not turn similarities into unsupported claims that the sellers are controlled by the same person.

## Begin with stable seller records

Create one record for each storefront. Capture the marketplace, storefront URL, visible seller name, seller identifier, business information, location, ratings, account age where available, product categories, and the date reviewed.

Names can change. Preserve the current presentation and any earlier names observed in case history.

## Collect comparable public signals

Useful comparison fields include:

- Public email addresses, phone numbers, and messaging handles.
- Store descriptions, policy text, and formatting patterns.
- Product photographs, watermarks, packaging, and size charts.
- Listing titles, identifiers, product combinations, and pricing.
- Shipping locations, stated business addresses, and return details.
- Linked websites and social accounts.
- Timing patterns and repeated spelling errors.

Only collect information relevant to the protection purpose. Do not seek private data or bypass access controls.

## Describe relationships precisely

Use terms such as “shared public phone number,” “same image file observed,” or “matching return address displayed.” Avoid labels such as “same operator” unless the evidence supports that conclusion and an authorized reviewer approves it.

Assign confidence to the observation, not to an unproven identity claim. For example, an exact phone-number match may be a strong link signal; similar photography alone may reflect copying or a common supplier.

## Preserve source-level evidence

Every link in an analysis map should point to the underlying capture. Record URLs, timestamps, screenshots, page text, and the method used to compare images or identifiers. Keep original images when perceptual similarity tools are used.

If a listing disappears, the case should still show how the seller and product were observed at the time.

## Use links to prioritize review

Link analysis can help teams identify:

1. Sellers that repeatedly list the same suspicious product family.
2. New storefronts reusing assets from earlier cases.
3. Accounts that share customer-contact routes.
4. Listings that may be appropriate for coordinated review.
5. Monitoring rules that should include new identifiers.

It does not replace product authentication, trademark analysis, or legal review.

## Report networks with caveats

Visual network charts can be persuasive, so include a legend explaining what each line represents. Separate observed facts, analytical inferences, and confirmed information. Allow reviewers to open the source evidence directly from the chart or case record.

Update the map when a seller changes details, a platform responds, or new evidence weakens an earlier hypothesis. Good analysis remains open to correction.
